No weight, no compression: the technique changes with gravity

On Earth the rescuer's weight does the work. On Mars it weighs 38 % and in cruise nothing. NOVA picks the technique by mission phase (cruise = microgravity, Mars = 0.38 g) or the one the crew sets, marks the compression point on the chest and counts compressions by watching the hands.

Microgravity CPR mode, live at the hackathon. Compression point, 100–120 per minute and 5–6 cm, the Evetts-Russomano technique with its source and the reaction arrow for anchoring.

  Counting compressions

Only hands within a quarter of the body size from the CPR point are used. Their vertical motion, normalized by body size, marks each compression. The rate is the median of the intervals over the last 5 s. A compression is good if the rate is between 100 and 120, and its depth between 5 and 6 cm when the camera sees from the side.

  When to switch rescuers

NOVA compares four plans over a 3-minute horizon (switch now, in 40 s, at 2:00 or keep going) and suggests the one that loses the fewest quality minutes. It assumes exponential fatigue that sets in sooner in microgravity (140 s) than on Mars (170 s) or Earth (240 s), counts 5 s hands-off per switch and moves it up if the rate drops below 100.

  The floating patient

In microgravity a push moves the patient away. NOVA measures how far the body drifts in one second (compressions go back and forth and cancel out), subtracts the phone's rotation and, above 12 cm/s, draws its path and asks to hold the patient before compressing.

  A voice that corrects little

NOVA speaks when something changes: the technique for this gravity, a patient drifting away or a rate out of range (“Faster. Aim for 100 to 120 a minute.”). It leaves at least 4 s between sentences. The first counter was tuned with a real CPR video: it counted 8 of 11 compressions with no false positives.

assumed The fatigue constants are design assumptions and do not come from a study. Depth is only estimated from the side. It still needs testing with a manikin and a rescuer.
